About this tool

Indigenous Peoples bring knowledge, skills and perspectives that strengthen the federal public service. This tool looks honestly at how well the public service is living up to its commitment to welcome, respect, support and fully include First Nations, Métis and Inuit employees: how they are represented, what they reported in the 2024 Public Service Employee Survey, and where the experience still falls short of that commitment. Every figure is drawn from published Government of Canada data; nothing is estimated.

It is organized around the five pillars of Many Voices, One Mind: A Pathway to Reconciliation, the whole-of-government strategy for Indigenous inclusion, so what you find here maps onto the framework departments already report against. This work is offered in the spirit of reconciliation, and with respect for First Nations, Métis and Inuit as distinct peoples rather than a single group.

The five pillars of Many Voices, One Mind

Many Voices, One Mind grew out of more than 2,100 responses from Indigenous employees gathered by the Interdepartmental Circles on Indigenous Representation, and is led by the Deputy Minister Champion for Federal Indigenous Employees. Its five pillars frame this page:

  • Creating a safe space. Psychological safety, freedom from discrimination and harassment, trust that concerns can be raised.
  • Cultural competency. Anti-racism, respect for difference, a workplace that understands Indigenous realities.
  • Training and development. Access to learning and support for growth.
  • Talent management. Recognition, advancement, and a real path toward and within leadership.
  • Recruitment and retention. Welcoming people in, and giving them reasons to stay.

How to read what you see

  • Gap bars and benchmarks. A dashed line is a benchmark (workforce availability, or the public-service figure). A red bar falls on the unfavourable side of it.
  • Direction. For the questions about experiencing discrimination, harassment, stress or racism's effect on mental health, a lower percentage is better. For every other question, higher is better. The colours handle this for you.
  • Distinctions. Where the numbers allow, First Nations, Métis and Inuit are shown separately. Inuit results are published for far fewer organizations because the population is small, and the page says so wherever that is the case.
  • Confidence. official data is transcribed directly from a Government of Canada source. interpretation marks a reading laid on top of the data.

Reading these numbers with care

These are screening signals, not conclusions. A gap shows where to look and ask; it does not by itself explain why. Numbers describe a workforce and cannot hold the fullness of anyone's experience; they are no substitute for listening to Indigenous employees directly, through sharing circles, networks and their own words. Where a group's count was too small to publish, the figure is left out rather than estimated; that silence reflects small numbers, not the absence of an issue, and the smallest communities are often the least visible in the data.

Recruitment and staffing

Representation is the result of who gets in and who moves up. These are the staffing stages where Indigenous candidates are most often lost, drawn from the Public Service Commission's audits and its staffing dashboard. The figures below are public-service-wide; your organization's own staffing data is what turns a pattern into an action, and the last section shows how to request it.

The recruitment funnel: where Indigenous applicants are shed official data

The Public Service Commission's audit of recruitment tracks each designated group from application to appointment. The "survival ratio" is appointment-stage representation divided by application-stage representation: 1.00 means a group ends where it started; below 1.00 means the group made up a smaller share of appointments than of applicants; it is not an individual selection rate.

0.83
Indigenous survival ratio through the recruitment funnel. Below 1.00: Indigenous applicants are lost between applying and being appointed.
Interviews
The audit found the interview stage drove the entire Indigenous drop; reference checks helped only women, and the appointment stage itself did not recover the loss.

Source: Public Service Commission, audit of employment equity representation in recruitment. The survival ratio compares the Indigenous share of applicants to the Indigenous share of those appointed.

Merit screening and automated pre-assessment

How candidates are screened before assessment shapes who ever reaches an interview. The Commission's audit of pre-assessment found that automated and organizational screening reduced representation for intersectional candidates specifically, including Indigenous people with disabilities, and that only about half of organizations had the accountability mechanisms in place to catch it.

  • Screening criteria, including how experience and education were judged and how citizenship status was treated, were identified as points where representation fell.
  • The losses compound for people who are Indigenous and also belong to another group, which a single-axis count can miss entirely.
  • The fix is local: examine your own organization's screening pass rates by Indigenous identity, and who sets and reviews the criteria.

Acting appointments: the untracked pipeline

Acting appointments are the main feeder to promotion, and they are the one staffing flow not in any Treasury Board table. Only the Public Service Commission's staffing dashboard reports them by group, and the National Joint Council's equity committee flagged equity representation in acting appointments as a 2024-25 consultation item.

If Indigenous representation is at availability overall but thinner in senior roles, the barrier has likely moved upstream into who gets acting opportunities, and at what level. That is testable: pull acting shares by Indigenous identity and subgroup for your organization.

Leadership

Representation overall and representation in the executive ranks are different questions. Indigenous employees are at or above workforce availability in the public service as a whole, which makes the senior-level picture the one to watch: gains at entry mean little if they do not reach the tables where decisions are made.

5.5%
Indigenous representation in the core public administration, above the 4.0% workforce-availability benchmark.
4.0%
Public-service-wide Indigenous workforce availability (not department-specific).

Overall representation above availability is real progress and worth stating plainly. The question it raises is distribution: whether that 5.5% is spread evenly across classifications and levels, or concentrated in junior and feeder groups while the executive cadre stays thinner. Acting appointments (previous section) are the mechanism that usually explains the difference.

Sources and method

Where the numbers come from

  • Workplace experience and subgroups. 2024 Public Service Employee Survey, Employment Equity derived-variable dataset (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat / Statistics Canada), via Open Government. Indigenous identity and the First Nations, Métis and Inuit breakdowns are respondents' self-identification.
  • Public-service-overall reference. 2024 PSES, organization-level results (subset 1).
  • Representation and workforce availability. Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat employment-equity data; Indigenous workforce availability of 4.0% is the benchmark used in federal employment-equity reporting.

How to read it, and its limits

  • For the two "experienced it" questions, lower is better; for the rest, higher is better. This is handled for you in the colours.
  • Comparisons across organizations rest on small Indigenous samples in smaller organizations; a single figure can move on a few responses.
  • Inuit results are published for only a handful of organizations; the page flags this wherever Inuit data is thin.
  • Suppressed cells are absence of published data, not absence of an issue.
  • These are screening signals to investigate, not findings or causal claims.
